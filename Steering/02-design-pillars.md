# Design Pillars

Every design decision should be tested against these. If a proposed feature violates a pillar, either the feature or the pillar needs to change deliberately — it shouldn't drift by accident.

### 1. One verb at every scale
The venture — fund something, roll odds shaped by who/what you commit, resolve into an outcome — is the single repeating decision pattern. Expeditions, trade runs, funding agitation, diplomatic gambits, and off-map dilemmas are all the same underlying system with different skins, costs and stakes. This is what makes the game solo-buildable: one system, built once, reused everywhere.

### 2. Behaviour and policy define identity, not menus
Leader traits and faction traits are earned, not chosen from a tree. Repeated raiding for slaves produces a "Slaver" identity; repeated welfare and refugee intake produces a "Socialist" identity; sustained tech/policy choices (e.g. long-form vs short-form media) shift population culture stats that, past a threshold, produce traits. No hand-authored branching trait trees.

### 3. Legible AI
AI factions run on the same trait engine, the same weighted-scoring action system, and the same venture mechanic as the player. Nothing the AI does should be a rule the player couldn't, in principle, read or predict from visible faction traits, strength and disposition. Telegraphing (grievance heat maps, threshold alerts, visible faction stats) is a design requirement, not a nice-to-have — it's what makes diplomacy and covert ops a puzzle rather than noise, and what makes collapses feel earned rather than arbitrary.

### 4. Mortality gives the climb weight
Characters are mortal (CK2-style, not CK3). Status gates relationships and succession; death is a real event with consequences, not a game-over. A crew, not a lone character, is the persistent unit — if a leader dies with no heir, another crew member succeeds by default. Dead leaders become 2D archive portraits; living ones use the active portrait system.

### 5. Open-ended and cyclical — success creates the next crisis
No fixed win state. A mature, stable state is not a victory lap; it's the setup for the next threat (succession crisis, internal faction grievance, off-map pressure). The collapse cycle is a core pillar, not an edge case: government type changes the risk profile (autocracy = fast/cheap/fragile succession; democracy = slower/safer/gridlock-prone), it doesn't remove the risk.
